Day 1The idea

Keep the look. Own the engine.

G-Force is great, but it's a closed program. You can't make it follow a DJ's tempo, add a new kind of picture, or send its output to OBS without workarounds.

So the plan was a brand-new app for the Mac, written from scratch in C++ on Apple's Metal graphics, that opens G-Force's own scene files and draws them the same way. Then, once it matched, keep going.

Three rules shaped everything after that:

Never look inside

G-Force's license forbids taking the program apart, so nobody did. Everything about how it behaves was learned by watching it from the outside.

Leave G-Force alone

Peter still uses it. Every experiment backed up its settings first, restored them after, and checked byte by byte that nothing had changed.

Match it, but not its flaws

Where copying G-Force would mean copying a flaw (jagged lines, banding), reforce offers both and Peter picks.

Days 1 – 3Black-box probing

Learning a program by watching it

The trick was writing tiny fake scenes, each one a single question, and feeding them to G-Force.

"Draw one dot, then clear the screen." "Fade this line and stop." A script launched G-Force, played the probe scenes, took screenshots, quit, and put everything back. Then the pixels were measured. Over 44 runs this turned up 46 numbered findings, and each became a rule reforce follows.

Days 1 – 2The Look card

Match it, or do it right?

Some of what the probes found are flaws: lost precision, jagged edges, muddy colors. reforce can copy each one exactly, which the comparison tests need, or do it properly. The control panel's Look card switches each one live, between "G-Force exact" and "Done right", so Peter could compare them on a real set. Peter picked "Done right" for all of them. Here are four, side by side.

How finely brightness is stored

These visuals fade the last frame a little and draw on top, over and over. G-Force keeps each pixel's brightness as a whole number from 0 to 255. Here both canvases fade 1.5% a frame: on the left, a dim pixel loses less than one step, rounds back to where it was and never goes dark, so a fog builds up. G-Force's fixed-step fade (finding #6) hides the worst of it, but its slow, soft trails still band and stick.

Thin lines

G-Force snaps every point of a thin line to a whole pixel (finding #32) and draws it one pixel tall, so slanted lines become staircases and slow movement crawls in little jumps. Done right, a line can sit between pixels, with soft edges and the same overall brightness. Both are magnified here so you can see the pixels.

See-through pictures

Sprites are pictures that float over the scene, and many have see-through edges. G-Force ignores the see-through part (finding #42) and shows whatever color happens to be stored there, often a box of leftover junk from whoever made the picture. Done right, see-through means not drawn.

Changing color schemes

When the slideshow moves to the next ColorMap, every color slides to its new value over a couple of seconds. G-Force mixes the red, green and blue numbers directly, which sags through darker, muddier colors halfway. Done right mixes in OKLab, a color space built around how eyes see, so brightness holds through the change. Each strip below is one color's journey, start on the left, end on the right.

Days 1 – 3Proving it

How close is "the same"?

"Looks right" isn't a test. So reforce got automatic ones: replay each probe in reforce frame by frame and measure the same pixels, and run every scene in both apps on a fixed test pattern and compare. A handful of leftover gaps are written down with their reasons.

And it's faster

Doing the same job, with the same scenes, reforce draws more frames per second than G-Force and uses about a fifth of the processor to do it.

Days 2 – 6Hearing the beat

Teaching it to count to four

G-Force reacts to loudness. A DJ wants pictures that land on the beat.

The first plan was to work the beat out from the sound alone, the way a person listening would. That's harder than it sounds. House music has breakdowns with no kick for a minute, and DJs nudge the tempo up and down mid-mix.

The secret weapon was Peter's own record collection: thousands of songs whose beats Peter had already lined up by hand in rekordbox. That became an answer key. reforce listened to every song, its guesses were scored, and it learned from its mistakes.

Hear hits100 times a second, look for sudden jumps in nine frequency bands.
Is it the beat?A small model trained on Peter's songs scores each hit: kick on the beat, or a snare in between?
Find the tempoLook for repeats one, two and four beats apart; prefer 80–160 BPM.
Hold steadyKeep counting through breakdowns; follow a DJ's 1–3 BPM nudge within about 2 seconds.
Tempo right (within 2%)77.5% → 94.7%
Locked on the beat42.2% → 89.7%

Scored on 600 songs it never trained on. In the 2,000 most recent songs: 96.5% and 92.8%. It also practised on 400 simulated DJ mixes, blends and tempo nudges included.

The weak spot was the moment a DJ nudges the tempo by 1–3 BPM. Around a nudge it was on the beat only 77.6% of the time, and each fix bought a couple of points.

Days 6 – 9Plan B: ask rekordbox

Stop guessing, start asking

Back on day 4, while planning the beat-synced dance videos, Claude had mentioned a long shot: a tool that reads the beat straight out of rekordbox's memory. Two days of squeezing the detector for another percent later, Peter remembered it and asked: could that work instead? rekordbox, the DJ software on Peter's laptop, already knows the beat (Peter lined those beats up by hand), the tempo and which song is playing. It just doesn't tell anyone.

An open-source tool called rkbx_link (by grufkork) does exactly that, by reading rekordbox's memory while it runs, finding the tempo and beat position, and broadcasting them over Ableton Link, which reforce already speaks. The catch: it relies on knowing exactly where in memory rekordbox keeps each value, those places move with every rekordbox release, and nobody had mapped Peter's version (7.2.19 on Apple Silicon).

rekordboxPlays the music. Knows tempo, beat, master deck and track titles, but keeps them to itself.
→
rkbx_link, patchedReads those values out of rekordbox's memory many times a second.
→
reforceGets the tempo and beat over Ableton Link, plus deck positions and titles over a private feed.
  1. Get permission to lookmacOS doesn't let one program read another's memory, so rkbx_link needs extra permission to do it, set up again after every rekordbox update.
  2. Treasure huntPlay a song, change the tempo, and watch which numbers in memory change with it. That found the tempo, the beat position, and the waveform analysis. The hard one was the master deck (the deck rekordbox treats as "the" tempo). One promising value turned out to change only when you pressed the MASTER button. Peter chose to find the real one rather than settle for a guess, and it was found.
  3. Following the trail to song titlesSearch memory for the text "Title: ", then work backwards through the chain of pointers from each deck to where its title lives, so it can be found again every time. That gives reforce the artist and title on every deck.
  4. Teaching rkbx_link to talk to reforceA new output sends each deck's position, beat and tempo to reforce, along with titles and Peter's tags. reforce combines that with the DDJ-1000's faders to work out which song the crowd is actually hearing.

What it unlocked. reforce follows rekordbox's own beat exactly. Phrase lines start from wherever Peter dropped each song in. And the song titles made something else possible.

Now playing, moved inside

Peter's streams show the artist, title and year of the current song in the corner. That caption came from history_server, a small program from Peter's own DJ tools. It checked rekordbox's play history once a second and served a web page, which OBS laid over the stream as a separate layer.

With titles coming straight from the decks, reforce now draws that caption itself, in the same style: bold capitals, bottom left, a fade-in, and a vocal track on its own line above.

Before: history_server in OBS

  • One more program to start, plus a browser layer in OBS
  • Showed the newest entry in rekordbox's history, which rekordbox writes on its own schedule
  • Had no idea which deck the crowd was hearing
  • Plain text on top of whatever was behind it

Now: drawn by reforce

  • Nothing extra to run: it's part of reforce's picture
  • Follows the mix: the channel faders and crossfader on Peter's DDJ-1000 say which deck you hear, and a deck takes over once it has been louder for 3 seconds
  • A soft shadow appears only where the visuals behind the text are bright, so it's always readable
  • Layered properly: it stays solid over the see-through output, sits above the camera and dancers, and the room lights never pick it up
Days 4 – 8Past G-Force

Then it stopped being a copy

Once the old scenes were right, Peter started coming up with new kinds of picture to put on top, and Claude built them.

Peter's idea

Dance videos that dance to your music

Peter collects dance clips: music-video dancers, silhouettes, VJ loops. The idea was to play them in reforce so the dancer hits the beat of whatever is playing, which means speeding each clip up or slowing it down to match the song. That turned out to be three problems.

  1. The frame timing liesMany clips had been converted between frame rates along the way, leaving repeated frames and an uneven stutter (2, 2, 1 frames apart, over and over). To a beat finder that stutter looks just like rhythm. So the first step finds and undoes it: two clips that measured 150 and 100 BPM turned out to be about 113 once cleaned up.
  2. The clips are silentSo the beat has to come from the dancing. A tool measures two kinds of motion: bounce, the body dipping on each beat (upper body, hips and legs), and hits, moves that stop or snap back. It fits a steady beat grid to both and keeps whichever is more confident.
  3. Slow motion looks choppyStretching a 25 fps clip to fit a slower song shows every frame. So each clip goes through RIFE, an AI that invents the frames in between, at four times the frame rate. Slowed down, it stays smooth. For seamless loops it even fills in the jump from the last frame back to the first.

Every clip gets a preview to check by eye and ear: the border flashes on each beat (red on the "one"), a click plays along, and the motion signals scroll past underneath.

Seamless VJ loops get their tempo from their exact length, so they never drift. This one by Beeple is exactly 40 beats long, so as it repeats, the beat carries straight across the seam.

In reforce, a synced clip follows the beat clock (rekordbox's beat when it's there, otherwise what reforce hears) and every loop starts on a downbeat. Not everything can be tamed: montages whose cuts ignore the music, stop-motion, and clips that change speed partway were left out.

Days 8 – 11The dance floor

Then they started dancing

Peter's next idea: cartoon dancers along the bottom of the stream, lit by the visuals, on a reflective disco floor. Peter shaped them from there too: the styles, the looks, mocap instead of hand-made moves, and letting stream guests join in.

The ones below aren't a video. This is reforce's actual dancer code, compiled to run in your browser, dancing real motion capture of professional dancers. Pick a style, dress them up, change the tempo.

Tempo 124 BPM

Each style cycles through its basic moves, then a full routine.

  • Real moves. 543 moves from AIST++, a research dataset of professional dancers in 3D, turned into size-free poses. Whole 16-bar routines start on 8- or 16-bar phrase lines, the way a dancer would.
  • Nine styles. House, pop, locking, breaking, disco, stomp, hip-hop, jazz and ballet, each dressed for its scene.
  • They notice each other. Dancers walk around the floor without bumping, using time-to-collision steering, and sometimes turn to watch one another.
  • A bit human. Arms lag the body slightly, every beat lands a few milliseconds differently, and ponytails and long hair swing on springs.

Stream guests: how a viewer joins the dance floor

People watching Peter's stream can put themselves on the dance floor. It takes three pieces working together: a web page on Peter's site, a small guest service in the cloud, and reforce on Peter's Mac.

The /dance pageOn the viewer's phone or laptop. Make a dancer, then tap "I'm here".
→
Guest serviceA Cloudflare Worker and a small database. Keeps each guest's dancer, check-ins and messages.
→
reforceAsks for new check-ins every 5 seconds and puts each guest on the floor.
  1. Make a dancer from a photoA guest can upload a selfie. The guest service sends it to Claude, which designs a cartoon dancer to match: hair, clothes, colors, glasses, beard. Claude answers in the same settings a reforce dancer file uses. The photo is never stored. Or the guest skips the photo and builds a dancer by hand from the menus.
  2. See the real thingThe preview on the page isn't an approximation. It's reforce's own dancer code compiled to run in the browser (the same trick as the dancers above), doing real motion-capture moves in the style they picked. What they see is exactly what will show up on stream.
  3. No accountsSaving stores the dancer and a name, and gives the browser a secret key. That key lets the guest come back to change their look or check in again, with no password.
  4. "I'm here"Tapping it while watching records a check-in. The page shows LIVE whenever reforce has been in touch in the last minute, so guests know Peter is on.
  5. Onto the floorEvery 5 seconds reforce asks the guest service for new check-ins, on a separate thread so the visuals never wait. Each one becomes a dancer with the guest's name over their head, dancing for 30 minutes. reforce remembers the last check-in it handled, so a restart never brings anyone on twice.
  6. Say somethingA guest can type a message of up to 140 characters, emoji included. It appears in a speech bubble above their dancer for 30 seconds.
Days 10 – 11Out of the screen

Into the room

Then Peter's ideas took reforce off the screen and into the room.

Camera backdrop. Peter's webcam can sit under the visuals, showing through wherever the picture is dark. This used to take a stack of OBS filters.

Seeing the person

With the camera inside reforce, reforce can look at it. Apple's Vision framework, built into macOS, finds the person in every camera frame: a silhouette (a cut-out mask of exactly where they are) and 19 body joints, head to ankles. On Peter's Mac that takes about 17 milliseconds, so it keeps up with every frame the camera sends. reforce can even hold the camera image back by that one step, so the cut-out lines up with the person exactly. The joints are smoothed so they sit still when you do but follow quickly when you move.

Then the visuals react. The silhouette is written into the same picture the FlowField keeps moving, so the scene carries it off, swirls it and colors it like anything else it draws.

Silhouette in the flow
Silhouette

A made-up dancer stands in for the camera here, so you don't need one. In reforce it's the real silhouette from Apple Vision.

  • Fill. The whole body glows into the scene and streams away with the flow.
  • Outline. Only the edge, so the scene keeps drawing your contour and carrying it off.
  • Motion. Only what moved since the last frame: stand still and nothing happens, dance and you leave trails.
  • Person in front. The camera image cuts through the visuals so you stand in front of them, while the dancers and the now-playing caption still stay in front of you.
Day 11Room lights

The wall joins in

Peter has 24 Nanoleaf triangles on the wall behind the decks. They used to copy the screen through Nanoleaf's own app. Peter wanted them to follow the visuals properly, and Claude suggested reforce could drive them itself.

Up to 30 times a second, reforce grabs the finished visuals, before the camera, the dancers and the captions are added, so those never tint the room. It shrinks the frame to about 120 pixels wide and stretches the panel layout over it. Each panel takes the color of the patch underneath, with the brightest pixels counting most, so even a thin bright line lights its panel. The colors go out over Wi-Fi 20 times a second. Panels brighten quickly and fade more slowly, so they flash with the music without flickering. Turning the lights off, or quitting, puts the panels back to whatever they were showing before.

The wall is the real layout of Peter's panels, read from the panels themselves, and it's colored the way reforce colors it.
